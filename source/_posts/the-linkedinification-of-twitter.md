Everyone on X suddenly became "humbled to announce" something. That's the same X whose feed used to be where people argued and thought out loud, and yes, sometimes they were wrong. Loudly, publicly, gloriously wrong.

A founder is humbled to announce a pivot.

A designer is humbled to announce a new shade of gray.

In a breakthrough that stunned the industry, an HR leader discovered that "employees are human."

A week later, the follow-up dropped: "Peer review is ongoing."

2,000 fellow HR leaders have already confirmed the findings with a comment.

To be fair, none of this is a crime. Being proud of your work is fine. Announcing things is fine. Even being humbled is fine, if you really are.

The problem is who's listening. It's an echo chamber that calls itself an audience.

I learned this in a theatre.

A friend invited me to a play that he had written and directed. I went, and I was happy for him.

Before the curtain went up, he pulled me aside and scanned the room like a general surveying his troops. "Look who's here," he said. He was proud of it. Almost the whole crowd was actors, art graduates and well-known directors. It was the scene.

The show ended and the room erupted. There was a standing ovation, with hugs in the aisles and "brilliant" and "so brave" all around.

Driving home, I couldn't shake this question:

Where was the person who'd never set foot in a theatre, bought a ticket, and walked out changed?

Would half that room have come if they didn't know him, or if he didn't know them? And that ovation, was it for the play, or was it a deposit? Scratch my back and I will scratch yours.

My friend walked away thinking he had made something great. Maybe he had. The problem is that nobody in that room could have told him otherwise. Everyone there had a reason to clap. Nobody there had a reason to be honest.

It was a closed loop, art made for artists.

## Why Applause From Peers Is Broken

Peer applause isn't worthless. It's just compromised.

When your audience is made up of people who:

- know you personally,
- work in your industry,
- and will need your likes tomorrow,

their applause measures your relationships. It doesn't measure your work. It's a social contract that looks like feedback.

The algorithm makes it worse. It rewards reciprocity, so the loops grow tighter, louder and more confident while drifting further from anyone real.

That's how you end up with a feed full of standing ovations for plays nobody outside the theatre would buy a ticket to.

## Back to the Feed

Now open X and look at the newest troupe in town: the vibe builders.

They don't write code anymore. They ship. You wish they shipped. They mostly ship posts about shipping.

> "Built a full SaaS in 4 minutes with Next.js, Vercel, Supabase and 12 AI agents. We are living in the future 🤯"

The replies come in fast:

> "Insane."  
> "This is the way."  
> "Bro is cooking 🔥"

All three are from people who posted their own four-minute SaaS yesterday.

Then comes the guest list:

> "🔥 Top 10 AI builders you NEED to follow in 2026 🔥"

Look closely. The ten names on the list are the same ten people who write these lists, which makes it less a recommendation than a seating chart.

And the ritual every week:

> "Building in public? Drop your project below 👇 I'll follow everyone who comments."

Four hundred comments roll in, four hundred follows go out, and four hundred people now have four hundred new followers who will never use their product. That's a follow train, and everyone on it is a passenger. Nobody is driving.

Then there's the bait:

> "Comment 'AGENT' and I'll DM you my secret prompt."

The prompt is "make it look like Stripe."

The demo is the best part. A silky screen recording with gradient backgrounds, glass cards and a cursor gliding across a dashboard full of perfect charts. The replies say "the UI is so clean." Nobody asks for a login, because there isn't one. The charts are hard-coded. The "Get Started" button goes nowhere. It's a stage set: a beautiful front door with nothing behind it.

And the build-in-public diary:

> "Day 112 of building in public. $0 MRR. 14k followers. Grateful for this community 🙏"

That last line says it all. The followers are real. The customers are imaginary. The community is the product.

Nowhere in that room is the one person who matters: someone with an actual problem, who would actually pay, and who would actually tell you it doesn't work.

## The Way Out

The way out isn't to stop announcing or to stop being proud. Ship it, post it, celebrate it, even be humbled to announce.

Just change who's in the room.

**Find the stranger.** Show your work to people who owe you nothing: a user, a customer, someone who's never heard your name. Their silence will teach you more than your circle's standing ovation.

**Ship the boring part.** The parts that are core to the business. Nobody claps for them. They're also the only parts that are real.

**Share what broke, and let it stay broken.** Not every story needs a triumphant third act, and not every burnout needs to end in a Series A.

**Distrust unanimous applause.** If everyone loved it, ask who wasn't there.

My friend's play might have been great. I still don't know, and neither does he.

That's the real cost of the echo chamber. You don't just lose honest feedback. You never find out if you were any good.

So the next time you're humbled to announce something, look around the theatre first.

Count the strangers.

## Where this comes from

At rulr we've spent twenty years building and rescuing PHP applications, and the pattern holds offline too. The projects that went wrong were never short of applause. They had enthusiastic stakeholders, polished demos, and a launch post everyone liked. What they lacked was a real user, early, with permission to say "this doesn't work."

So that's how we run things. We ship the boring core before the gradient dashboard. We put unfinished software in front of actual customers in week two, not month six. And when a client's friends love the prototype, we ask the same question we'd ask in that theatre: who wasn't in the room?
